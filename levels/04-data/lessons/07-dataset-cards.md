# Lesson 7: Dataset Cards

## Core Idea

A dataset card is the document that lets someone decide whether to use your data
without reading it. That is the whole job, and it sets a sharper standard than
"documentation": the card must be enough for a stranger to conclude **"this is
not suitable for what I want"** and walk away.

Cards written as marketing fail that test. Every dataset has limits, and a card
that lists only sources and schema leaves the reader to discover them by
training on it. The load-bearing section is Limitations, and if it is the
shortest one on the page, the card is not finished.

The second reason to write one is closer to home. A card is a claim you have to
defend against your own build output. When the card says 152 cleaned rows and
the metrics say 149, someone has to reconcile it — and that argument is the
point. Generating the card from the build is how you make the claim keep pace
with the data.

## The Card This Repo Generates

`python3 -m datasets.strongbench` emits
[`dataset-card.md`](../../../datasets/strongbench/dataset-card.md) with eight
sections. What each is *for*:

| Section | The reader's question |
| --- | --- |
| Intended Use | should I use this at all? |
| Sources | where did it come from, and can I trust that? |
| Schema | what shape are the rows? |
| Cleaning | what was removed, by what rule? |
| Metrics | how much of what? |
| Limitations | what will go wrong if I use it anyway? |
| Privacy | can I share this? |
| Level 5 Use Case | which split for which job? |

The first line does more work than any other:

```text
It is a teaching dataset, not production financial data.
```

Scope stated before anything else. A reader deciding whether this fits their
problem now has their answer in one sentence and can stop.

## Sources Are Paths, Not Descriptions

```text
- Level 1 trace demonstrations from `examples/strongbench-expense-agent/traces/level-1.jsonl`.
- Level 3 annotated benchmark failures from `evals/operations/strongbench/annotated-failures.jsonl`.
- Synthetic gap-targeted examples generated by `python3 -m datasets.strongbench`.
```

Each source names a file or a command, so a reader can go and look. "Curated
from agent interactions" would be the same sentence with the verifiability
removed.

The third line is the one to copy. Declaring that 100 of 152 rows are
**synthetic and template-generated** is the disclosure most likely to change a
reader's mind, and it is voluntary. A card that omits it is not wrong in any
checkable way and is still misleading.

## Limitations Is The Section That Matters

```text
Synthetic examples are template-generated and should not be used to claim real
model improvement without held-out benchmark evaluation. Correction targets are
compact oracle summaries, not full human-authored ideal answers.
```

Two admissions, both costly, both necessary.

The first bounds what a result from this data can support. Two-thirds of the
rows come from templates, so a model that improves on them may have learned the
template. The card says so, and names the check that would settle it.

The second is subtler and easy to miss: correction targets are *compact oracle
summaries*, not what a careful human would actually write. Train on them and you
get a model that produces terse, correct-shaped answers — which will look like
success on any grader that checks fields, and like a regression to anyone reading
the output. That is exactly the kind of surprise a card exists to prevent.

Write your Limitations section by asking what you would feel obliged to mention
if a colleague told you they were about to fine-tune on it. Then write that down
instead of saying it.

## Metrics Are Copied From The Build

```text
- raw rows: 155
- cleaned rows: 152
- rejected rows: 3
- splits: {"dev": 47, "heldout": 27, "train": 78}
- sources: {"failure_correction": 30, "level1_trace": 22, "synthetic_gap_target": 100}
```

These are not typed by hand. `render_dataset_card` reads the same metrics dict
the build wrote, so the card cannot drift from the data it describes.

That property is the difference between a card that stays true and one that was
true once. Hand-written counts are correct on the day of writing and wrong from
the next rebuild onward, silently, in a document whose entire purpose is to be
trusted without checking.

The split line also encodes the contamination rule from Lesson 6 in a form a
reader can verify: 27 heldout rows, and the Level 5 use case that follows tells
them what heldout is for.

## Privacy Deserves A Line Even When It Is Boring

```text
All examples use fictional StrongBench fixtures. No personal, customer, or
production data is included.
```

Nothing sensitive is present, and the card says so explicitly. Absence of a
privacy section reads as an unanswered question; a reader cannot distinguish "no
personal data" from "nobody checked". One sentence converts the second into the
first.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Limitations shorter than Sources.** The reader's actual risk is in the
  section you skimped.
- **Hand-written counts.** True once, then quietly wrong forever.
- **Sources described rather than located.** "Curated from interactions" cannot
  be checked.
- **No statement of intended use.** Every reader assumes their use is the
  intended one.
- **Omitting the synthetic fraction.** The single most decision-relevant fact
  about this dataset, and it is voluntary to disclose.
- **No privacy line.** Silence reads as unchecked, not as clean.
- **A card written after the fact.** Written at the end, it describes what you
  built; written alongside, it constrains it.

## Exercise

Open [`dataset-card.md`](../../../datasets/strongbench/dataset-card.md) and
[`metrics.json`](../../../datasets/strongbench/metrics.json).

1. Which single sentence in the card would most change the decision of someone
   planning to fine-tune on this data, and which section is it in?
2. The card reports 155 raw and 152 cleaned. Which builder function guarantees
   those numbers match the data, and what would you check if you suspected a
   card had been hand-edited?
3. The Limitations section warns that correction targets are "compact oracle
   summaries, not full human-authored ideal answers." Describe the specific
   failure a team would hit if they ignored that line.

Check your answer:

```text
1. "Synthetic examples are template-generated and should not be used to claim
   real model improvement without held-out benchmark evaluation," in
   Limitations. 100 of 152 rows are synthetic, so a gain measured on this data
   may be template-learning rather than capability. Sources hints at it by
   naming the generator; Limitations is where it becomes a warning.

2. render_dataset_card reads the metrics dict the build produced, so the card's
   numbers are generated rather than typed. To check for hand-editing, re-run
   python3 -m datasets.strongbench and diff the card — a generated card is
   byte-identical after a rebuild, and any difference is either a hand edit or
   a real data change, both of which you want to see.

3. They fine-tune on terse oracle summaries and get a model that emits terse,
   schema-correct answers. Deterministic graders pass, because the fields are
   right. Rubric and human review regress, because the answers stop explaining
   themselves. The metrics improve while the product gets worse — and the team
   only finds out from users, because the target format was the thing that
   changed and nothing was measuring it.
```

Then rebuild the dataset and diff the card. Byte-identical output is the
property that makes it trustworthy; if you can hand-edit a card without the
build noticing, it is prose, not documentation.

## Checkpoint

You are ready to move on when your card states intended use in its first lines,
locates its sources as paths, generates its counts from the build, and has a
Limitations section you would be comfortable having quoted back to you.
